# Herbal-product market access in key export markets (EU, US)

An Ayurvedic product legally sold in India is not automatically sellable abroad. Each export market decides for itself whether the product is a medicine, a food supplement or a cosmetic, and that decision drives everything else.

## European Union: Directive 2004/24/EC (THMPD)
Traditional Herbal Medicinal Products Directive, fully in effect from 30 April 2011. Creates a simplified "traditional-use registration" (THR) that does not require full clinical trials.
- Evidence needed: at least 30 years of traditional use for the claimed indication, of which at least 15 years must normally have been within the EU.
- Restricted to products suitable for use without a doctor's supervision, at a specified strength and dosage, taken orally, externally or by inhalation.
- The 15-years-in-the-EU condition is the practical barrier for Ayurveda. A formulation with centuries of documented use in India but little EU market history may still fail to qualify, which is why many Ayurvedic products enter the EU as food supplements rather than as registered herbal medicines.

## United States: DSHEA 1994
Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act. Ayurvedic products typically enter as dietary supplements, regulated as a category of food, not as drugs.
- No FDA pre-market approval for safety or effectiveness. The burden sits with the FDA to show a marketed product is unsafe.
- New Dietary Ingredient (NDI) notification is required, with reasonable evidence of safety, for ingredients not marketed in the US before 15 October 1994.
- Structure/function claims (effect on normal body function) are permitted. Disease claims (treat, cure or prevent a disease) are not, and making one reclassifies the product as an unapproved drug.
- Mandatory disclaimer on structure/function claims: "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."

Implication for Ayurveda:
- The same product often has a different legal identity in each market: a classical medicine in India, a food supplement in the US, and either a registered traditional herbal medicine or a food supplement in the EU.
- Claim discipline is the recurring trap. A therapeutic claim that is acceptable on an Indian classical-medicine label can make the product an unapproved drug in the US, and the domestic advertising limits under the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act still apply in parallel (see `magic_remedies_act.md`).
- Market access is separate from IP. Clearing THMPD or DSHEA says nothing about patent, trademark or ABS obligations, and does not remove the GRATK disclosure duty on any linked patent filing.
